Section 1 · Notebook §2–3

The Laplacian is “you minus your neighbourhood average”

The notebook’s first answer to “where did the Laplacian come from?” is a limit. Average $f$ over a small sphere around a point, subtract the value at the centre, and rescale by $2d/r^2$. What survives as $r\to 0$ is always $\Delta f$.

Theorem 2.6 (surface) and its ball counterpart
$$\Delta f(\mathbf x_0)=\lim_{r\to0}\frac{2d}{r^{2}}\Big(\langle f\rangle_{\partial B_r}-f(\mathbf x_0)\Big)=\lim_{r\to0}\frac{2(d+2)}{r^{2}}\Big(\langle f\rangle_{B_r}-f(\mathbf x_0)\Big)$$

$\langle f\rangle_{\partial B_r}$ is the mean over the sphere’s surface (the notebook’s meanSurfaceValue), $\langle f\rangle_{B_r}$ the mean over the solid ball (meanVolumeValue, §6), and $d$ is the dimension.

The error shrinks like $r^2$: the next term of the expansion is $\tfrac{r^4}{2d\,\cdot\,\ldots}\Delta^2 f$ (Section 3). For a harmonic $f$ such as $1/r$ or $x^2-y^2$ the estimate is already at roundoff level. The means are computed with Gauss–Legendre quadrature on the sphere, the numerical counterpart of the notebook’s SurfaceIntegrate.

Why the Taylor series? The notebook notes that a direct SurfaceIntegrate of an arbitrary $f$ is usually out of reach. Expanding $f$ first turns the problem into integrating monomials, and those integrals have closed forms (Section 2). This is the “universal paradigm”: rely only on commutativity, linearity, differentiability, Taylor form and a few Gamma-function identities.

Section 2 · Notebook §1, §4

Each Taylor term integrates to zero or a clean number

The 3-variable Taylor polynomial is a sum of terms $\dfrac{f^{(\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\alpha_3)}(\mathbf x_0)}{\alpha_1!\,\alpha_2!\,\alpha_3!}(x-x_0)^{\alpha_1}(y-y_0)^{\alpha_2}(z-z_0)^{\alpha_3}$. Averaged over a sphere, only terms whose exponents are all even survive.

Theorem 4.3

If any $\alpha_i$ is odd, $\displaystyle\oint (x-x_0)^{\alpha_1}(y-y_0)^{\alpha_2}(z-z_0)^{\alpha_3}\,dS = 0$. The sphere is symmetric under $x-x_0\mapsto -(x-x_0)$, so odd terms cancel.

Theorem 4.5.2 (all even, d = 3)

The mean of the full Taylor term is $\dfrac{1}{(1+|\alpha|)!}\dbinom{|\alpha|/2}{\alpha_1/2,\,\alpha_2/2,\,\alpha_3/2}\,f^{(\alpha)}(\mathbf x_0)\,r^{|\alpha|}$.

In[ ] Pick a multi-index α and integrate its monomial

General $d$: for all-even $\alpha$ with $|\alpha|=2k$, $\;\langle \prod x_i^{\alpha_i}\rangle_{S^{d-1}_r} = r^{2k}\,\dfrac{\prod_i(\alpha_i-1)!!}{d(d+2)\cdots(d+2k-2)}$. The notebook’s Factorial2 form (§4.6) is this formula at $d=3$. Its Factorial form (§4.5) comes from the identity $\Gamma\!\big(1+\tfrac{\alpha}{2}\big)=\sqrt{\pi}\,\alpha!/(2^{\alpha}(\alpha/2)!)$ applied to the Gamma-function result of SurfaceIntegrate.

Note 2.1.1 Bookkeeping check from §1.3: Coefficient[series, f^(1,3,2)] = (x−x₀)(y−y₀)³(z−z₀)²/12

The Taylor coefficient of $f^{(\alpha)}$ is always $1/\prod\alpha_i!$. For $(1,3,2)$ that is $1/(1!\,3!\,2!)=1/12$. The surface integral then removes this term because $\alpha_1=1$ is odd.

Section 3 · Notebook §2.2–2.3, §5.4, §6

The sphere mean is a power series in Δ

Group the surviving even terms by total degree $2k$. Each group has exactly the multinomial pattern of $\Delta^k f$ (Section 4). The whole mean therefore collapses into one series in powers of the Laplacian.

Theorem 5.4, general d
$$\langle f\rangle_{\partial B_r}=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{r^{2k}\,\Delta^k f(\mathbf x_0)}{2^k\,k!\;d(d+2)\cdots(d+2k-2)},\qquad \langle f\rangle_{B_r}=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{r^{2k}\,\Delta^k f(\mathbf x_0)}{2^k\,k!\;(d+2)(d+4)\cdots(d+2k)}$$
In[ ] Coefficients of $r^{2k}\Delta^k f$, exact rationals

Read the $k=1$ column backwards to get Section 1. With $\langle f\rangle - f_0 = \frac{r^2}{2d}\Delta f + O(r^4)$, multiplying by $2d/r^2$ and letting $r\to0$ leaves $\Delta f$. In the notebook’s $d=3$ output, the surface coefficients are $1/6, 1/120, 1/5040$ and the ball coefficients $1/10, 1/280, \ldots$. They match the table.

Section 4 · Notebook §5.1–5.3

Δⁿ expands exactly like (x + y + z)ⁿ

Treat $\partial_x^2,\partial_y^2,\partial_z^2$ as commuting symbols. Then $\Delta^n=(\partial_x^2+\partial_y^2+\partial_z^2)^n$ expands like a polynomial. The notebook’s exponentsToDervs[ ] makes this concrete by turning the monomial $x^a y^b z^c$ into $f^{(2a,2b,2c)}$.

Theorem 5.1
$$\Delta^{n} f=\sum_{k_1+\cdots+k_d=n}\binom{n}{k_1,\ldots,k_d}\,f^{(2k_1,\ldots,2k_d)}$$
In[ ] exponentsToDervs /@ MonomialList@Expand[(x+y+z)^n]

This is the second half of Theorem 5.4. The sphere integral produces the factor $\binom{k}{\alpha/2}/(\ldots)$ for each even term, and $\Delta^k$ produces the same multinomial. So the integrated Taylor series regroups term by term into powers of $\Delta$, with no leftover terms.

Section 5 · Notebook §7–7.4

Replace the sphere with 2d points

Discretely, average over the $2d$ points where the coordinate axes cross a sphere of radius $h$. Taylor expansion gives $\sum_{i}\big[f(\mathbf x_0+h\mathbf e_i)+f(\mathbf x_0-h\mathbf e_i)\big]=2d\,f_0+h^2\Delta f+O(h^4)$. This is the familiar 5-point (2D) and 7-point (3D) stencil.

In[ ] Stencil estimate (Σ f(x₀ ± h eᵢ) − 2d f₀)/h²
stencil errorslope 2 guide

For very small $h$ the error turns back up: $f_0$ is subtracted from nearly equal numbers and then divided by $h^2$, so floating-point roundoff takes over. Symbolic computation, as in the notebook, avoids this.

In[ ] §7.1 vs §7.3: rotate the stencil axes, or skew them

Take two stencil directions $\mathbf u,\mathbf v$ in the plane. To second order, $\sum_{\pm}f(\mathbf x_0\pm h\mathbf u)+f(\mathbf x_0\pm h\mathbf v)-4f_0 = h^2\,\nabla^{\!\top}\!\big(\mathbf u\mathbf u^{\!\top}+\mathbf v\mathbf v^{\!\top}\big)\nabla f$. This is the Laplacian only when $M=\mathbf u\mathbf u^{\!\top}+\mathbf v\mathbf v^{\!\top}=I$, that is, when the axes are orthonormal.

Theorem 7.4

Non-orthonormal axes do not produce the Laplacian as the limit of a mean. A cross term $2M_{12}\,\partial_x\partial_y f$ appears, and the diagonal weights become unequal. Rotations keep $M=I$, which is why the Laplacian looks the same in every orthonormal frame. This is the “orthonormal mandate” from the notebook’s abstract.

Section 6 · Notebook §7.5, §8

Why 1/r passes the test and 1/r² does not

The notebook checks $1/r$, $1/r^2$ and $1/r^3$ with symbolic sphere means and spherical-mesh sums. One formula explains all three results: for $f=r^{p}$ in $d$ dimensions (away from the origin), $\Delta r^{p}=p\,(p+d-2)\,r^{p-2}$.

The §8.2 value, 3.538, does not “blow up”. It is an accurate estimate of the true, nonzero Laplacian $2/|\mathbf x|^4 = 2/0.75^2 = 3.556$, off by 0.5% from mesh discretisation. $1/r^2$ is simply not harmonic in 3D. In 3D only $p=0$ and $p=-1$ give $\Delta r^p=0$. The “Indeterminate” in §7.5.1 has a different cause, explained in Section 9.

At the origin. $1/r$ is harmonic everywhere except $r=0$. There, $\Delta\frac{1}{r}=-4\pi\,\delta^{3}(\mathbf x)$, which is the source term of Coulomb’s law and Newtonian gravity, $\Delta\phi=-\rho/\varepsilon_0$. So the mean-value property fails for any sphere that encloses the origin.

Section 7 · Notebook §9 and the Coda

On a graph, “neighbours” are just the edges

Drop the geometry and keep only who sits next to whom. The graph Laplacian (Mathematica’s KirchhoffMatrix) is $L=D-A$, so $(Lf)_i=\deg(i)\,f_i-\sum_{j\sim i}f_j$. Divided by the degree, as in notebook §9.4.2, it reads $f_i-\text{mean of neighbours}$, the stadium story from the Coda. The sign is opposite to $\Delta$: $L$ approximates $-h^2\Delta$ on a grid.

Spectrum of L (eigenvalues, 0 … λₘₐₓ)

“Diffuse” applies the heat equation $f\leftarrow f-\tau Lf$ with $\tau=0.9/\deg_{\max}$, which is stable because $\lambda_{\max}\le 2\deg_{\max}$. “Make interior average” pins the outer vertices of the grid to a ramp (left 1, right −1) and relaxes every inner vertex to its neighbours’ mean. The result is a discrete harmonic function: $(Lf)_i=0$ in the interior.

Mesh (cotangent) Laplacian
$$(\Delta f)_i\approx\frac{1}{2A_i}\sum_{j\sim i}\big(\cot\alpha_{ij}+\cot\beta_{ij}\big)(f_j-f_i)$$

On a triangle mesh, the edge $ij$ is weighted by the cotangents of the two angles opposite it, and $A_i$ is the vertex’s share of area. On a uniform right-triangle grid these weights reduce to the 5-point stencil. Plain graph weights (all 1) ignore geometry, which is why the notebook’s volume-mesh graph values in §9.4 converge only roughly.

Section 8 · Reference

One operator, many forms

Each form below is the same idea, “value minus local average” to leading order, written for a different setting. Use this section as a reference sheet. Every card has a TA prompt.

Where it shows up in physics

EquationFormWhat Δ does there
Laplace$\Delta u=0$equilibrium: every point equals its local average
Poisson$\Delta \phi=-\rho/\varepsilon_0$sources push a point away from its average
Heat / diffusion$u_t=\kappa\,\Delta u$a point drifts toward its neighbours’ average
Wave$u_{tt}=c^2\Delta u$restoring force ∝ difference from the average
Schrödinger$i\hbar\psi_t=-\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\Delta\psi+V\psi$kinetic energy = curvature of ψ
Helmholtz$\Delta u+k^2u=0$eigenfunctions: vibration modes, the graph spectrum of §7

The notebook’s answer to “why is the Laplacian ubiquitous?” is that any rotation-invariant, linear, second-order, local comparison of a value with its surroundings must be a multiple of $\Delta$. Sections 1–5 build that fact out of Taylor series.

Section 9 · For the author and careful readers

Notes on the notebook

The notebook asks readers to report bugs in the code or theory. These notes come from reading it closely. One resolves TODO 2.9; the others point to cells worth a second look.

TODO 2.9 $r^{(d-1)}$ or $r^{2}$?

Both appear in the literature because they normalise different quantities. Using the mean (divided by the area), the factor is $2d/r^2$ in every dimension, which is what the notebook’s code computes. Using the raw surface integral $\oint(f-f_0)\,dS=\omega_{d-1}r^{d-1}\big(\langle f\rangle-f_0\big)$, you have to divide by the area $\omega_{d-1}r^{d-1}$ first:

$$\Delta f(\mathbf x_0)=\lim_{r\to0}\frac{2d}{\omega_{d-1}\,r^{d+1}}\oint_{\partial B_r}\!(f-f_0)\,dS .$$

A formula that writes $r^{d-1}$ next to the mean, rather than the integral, is correct only for $d=3$, where $r^{d-1}=r^2$. That coincidence is probably why the two versions look interchangeable. Theorem 3.1, with $A$ for the area and $r^2$, is the consistent form. The 4D cell in §3.2 confirms $2d/r^2$ with $d=4$.

§7.5.1 Why $1/r^3$ gave Indeterminate

That cell divides the surface integral by $\text{Area}\cdot\varepsilon^{3}=4\pi\varepsilon^{5}$, not by $\text{Area}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}/(2d)$. The leading term of the integral is $\tfrac{4\pi\varepsilon^4}{6}\Delta f$, so the quotient behaves like $\Delta f/(6\varepsilon)$ and diverges, giving “Indeterminate”. With the Theorem 3.1 normalisation, $\tfrac{2d}{4\pi\varepsilon^{4}}\oint(f-f_0)\,dS\to 6/|\mathbf x_0|^5$, which is the exact $\Delta r^{-3}$. In §7.5.2 the same extra $1/\varepsilon$ is harmless only because $\Delta(1/r)=0$ makes the leading term vanish.

§7.1, §7.3 r0={x0,y0,y0}

The centre is set to $(x_0,y_0,y_0)$. It looks like a typo for $(x_0,y_0,z_0)$. The conclusions still hold, because $f$ is abstract and any centre works. Using {x0,y0,z0} would keep the outputs consistent with 𝒞enter and with the rest of the notebook.

§3.2 series3 vs series2

The 4D cell builds series3 but integrates series2 - f[x0,y0,z0,t0], which is never defined. The stored CloudSymbol hides this. A fresh evaluation would fail or integrate the wrong expression unless series2 is renamed to series3.

§8.2, §9.4.3 “blows up” for $1/r^2$

The text says the $1/r^2$ estimates “remain far from 0 or blow up”. A more precise reading is that they converge to the nonzero value $2/|\mathbf x|^4$ (3.556 at $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$; the mesh gave 3.538). On the torus graph, the large values come from vertices close to the origin, where $2/|\mathbf x|^4$ is large. See Section 6.
